# Mining Existing Material

*For people who didn't start from zero — an alternative on-ramp,
alongside `start-here/participant-worksheet.md`*

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Is This You?

The rest of this kit assumes you're writing your first stories from
memory, this week, from scratch. If that's you, stop here and go start
`start-here/participant-worksheet.md` instead — there's nothing on this
page you need yet.

This page is for a narrower case: you already have old material —
decks you've presented, docs you've written, notes from sessions you've
led — from **before** you had any system for capturing your own
expertise. If you've been doing the work for years, you're probably not
starting from nothing. You're starting from a hard drive that already
holds your stories, just not in a shape anyone can use yet.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# The One Rule This Cannot Break

**Never invent what the source doesn't say.** An old slide's bullet
point is usually the *lesson*, not the moment that taught it — "share
failures, not just wins" is a conclusion, not a story. If the actual
moment behind a conclusion isn't written down anywhere in your old
material, the honest move is to leave it blank or flag it for you to
fill in from memory — never to write a plausible-sounding moment to
close the gap. A made-up story that sounds right is worse than an empty
field, because nothing will ever tell you it's fake.

This is the same rule the whole kit runs on — **your words, not a
polished stand-in for them** — just aimed at a document instead of a
conversation.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# How To Use This

Give an AI assistant (Claude, ChatGPT, whatever you have) this page and
your old material — a deck, a doc, a folder of notes — and ask it to:

1.  **Read the source closely**, including speaker notes and headers,
    not just headline bullets. The real story is often in the notes,
    not the slide.
2.  **Look for concrete moments** underneath every conclusion: a
    specific event, a decision, an exchange with a real person, a
    mistake, advice you gave more than once. A slide that only states a
    takeaway with nothing concrete behind it anywhere in the source
    isn't a story yet.
3.  **Draft using `templates/story-card.md`'s exact shape** — one card
    per moment, in your source's own words for "What happened." If
    "What it revealed" isn't actually stated anywhere in the source,
    write "Not stated in source — needs your read," not an invented
    lesson.
4.  **Note anything that repeats** across different old decks or docs —
    that's the seed of a `templates/pattern-hunch.md` entry, but only
    once you've reread it yourself and confirmed it's really the same
    lesson in a different context, not the same deck reused twice.
5.  **List anything the source references but doesn't actually
    capture** — a story mentioned by name with no detail behind it —
    under its own honest heading, so you know what still needs you
    directly instead of mistaking a gap for something finished.
6.  **Save the output as one new file** — don't overwrite anything in
    `templates/`. Something like `mined-stories.md` in wherever you
    keep your notes is enough.

# What You Get, and What You Still Have To Do

What comes back is a **first pass**, not finished stories. Read every
card against the source it cites before you trust it. Every card that
survives that check becomes real material for
`templates/thirty-day-capture-plan.md` — you're just starting that plan
with a head start instead of a blank page.

Nothing here skips the rest of the kit. It skips the *remembering*
part of week one, not the reading, the judging, or the deciding what's
actually true.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# One Meaningful Next Step

Pick the single oldest deck or document you can find — not your whole
archive, just one. Run this on that alone, and read what comes back
with your own eyes before you trust the shape of it on anything bigger.
